# Pixel Panic Art Bible

## Purpose
This art bible defines the visual direction for Pixel Panic, a modern online multiplayer pixel-art arcade game. It is written for pixel artists, environment artists, UI artists, VFX artists, and art directors who need a shared standard for style, readability, and content production.

The goal is to create a world that feels nostalgic at a glance, but modern in composition, clarity, animation, and polish. The art must support competitive play, mobile readability, fast match recognition, and strong theme variety.

---

## 1. Art Direction Summary
Pixel Panic should look like a premium arcade game that has been reimagined for the browser era. The art direction combines:
- Clear pixel silhouettes.
- Strong color separation.
- Bold thematic environments.
- Modern lighting and VFX discipline.
- Highly readable combat feedback.

### Core Visual Pillars
1. **Readable at speed**: Players must understand the battlefield instantly.
2. **Retro-inspired, not retro-locked**: Pixel art is the medium, not a limitation.
3. **Expressive and colorful**: Characters and maps should feel lively and memorable.
4. **Theme-first production**: Each map theme needs a distinct visual identity.
5. **Competitive clarity over decoration**: Gameplay readability always wins.

### Art Tone
- Playful, energetic, and polished.
- Bright enough to feel arcade-like.
- Dark enough to support contrast and combat visibility.
- Whimsical without becoming childish.
- Stylized rather than realistic.

## 2. Pixel Style
### 2.1 Overall Pixel Philosophy
Pixel Panic should use **clean, modern pixel art** with controlled pixel density and sharp silhouettes. The visual style should avoid excessive dithering, noisy textures, or cluttered sprite construction.

### 2.2 Pixel Density
- Characters should be built at a consistent scale so they read clearly in motion.
- Environment tiles may be denser than character sprites, but not so dense that they compete with combat readability.
- Important gameplay objects such as bombs, hazards, pickups, and ice effects should use bold shapes rather than ultra-fine detail.

### 2.3 Pixel Clarity Rules
- Avoid visual noise in the center of the playfield.
- Use strong shape language first, decoration second.
- Limit micro-detail on moving objects.
- Favor clear color blocking and contour definition.
- Use a consistent pixel grid for all gameplay-critical assets.

### 2.4 Retro Inspiration
The game should evoke classic arcade and console-era pixel art through:
- Chunky shapes.
- Bright accent colors.
- Character charm.
- Clear tile-based world structure.
- Simple but expressive animation poses.

It should not imitate old hardware constraints too literally. The result should feel contemporary and intentionally authored.

---

## 3. Character Proportions
### 3.1 Proportion Target
Characters should be slightly chibi-leaning but not toy-like. They should be compact enough for combat readability and expressive enough to support personality and cosmetics.

### 3.2 Recommended Proportion Style
- Large head relative to body for readability.
- Short torso with strong shoulders or silhouette emphasis.
- Small but expressive limbs.
- Hands and feet should remain readable but not over-detailed.
- Keep weapon or bomb-related props visually integrated into the silhouette.

### 3.3 Silhouette Rules
Every character must be recognizable from a distance using silhouette alone.

### 3.4 Readability Rules
- Distinct head shapes and accessory profiles.
- Avoid overly thin limbs.
- Use exaggerated stance differences between character types.
- Strong color accents should support silhouette recognition, not replace it.

### 3.5 Character Read Targets
Characters should read clearly in three states:
- Idle and lobby pose.
- Fast movement.
- Combat action with effects around them.

---

## 4. Character Style Language
### 4.1 Base Style
Characters should feel like arcade heroes with modern polish. Their designs should blend compact proportions, vivid color identity, and slightly exaggerated energy.

### 4.2 Design Rules
- Each character should have a primary color family and one or two accent colors.
- Accessories should reinforce role or personality.
- Clothing should avoid excessive texture; shape and color carry the design.
- Visible gadgets, gloves, helmets, capes, or headgear can communicate playstyle.

### 4.3 Cosmetic Compatibility
Characters must be built to support skins, recolors, hats, emotes, trails, victory poses, and seasonal overlays without destroying their base readability.

---

## 5. Animation Style
### 5.1 Animation Philosophy
Animations should feel lively, elastic, and readable. Pixel Panic should have enough motion to feel premium, but every action must remain understandable at a glance.

### 5.2 Animation Traits
- Snappy starts and releases.
- Clear anticipation before important actions.
- Strong pose silhouettes at key frames.
- Slight exaggeration in idle and victory states.
- Minimal unnecessary in-between blur.

### 5.3 Frame Guidance
- Use compact frame counts for gameplay-critical actions.
- Reserve more frames for special attacks, victory poses, and seasonal showcase animations.
- Idle motion should be subtle enough to avoid visual fatigue.

### 5.4 Animation Principles
- Movement should feel responsive before it feels fancy.
- Combat actions should telegraph just enough to be fair.
- Idle loops should make characters feel alive but not distracting.
- Effects should complement the animation, not hide it.

### 5.5 Recommended Animation Set
Each character should have:
- Idle.
- Walk or run.
- Turn or direction shift.
- Bomb placement or attack.
- Hit reaction.
- Stun or freeze.
- Elimination.
- Victory pose.
- Defeat pose.
- Emote reactions if supported.

---

## 6. Environment Art Direction
### 6.1 World Building Goal
Environments should feel like compact arcade battle arenas with thematic identity. Each map should communicate its theme instantly while still remaining compatible with fast multiplayer gameplay.

### 6.2 Environment Style Rules
- Strong foreground readability.
- Background detail should never overpower playable tiles.
- Tiles and props should be modular and reusable.
- The environment should support destruction, hazards, ice, lighting, and clear lane structure.
- Color and shape should communicate the theme more than texture complexity.

### 6.3 Environment Depth
Use three visual layers where appropriate:
- **Gameplay layer**: the playable tile grid and obstacles.
- **Midground layer**: props, signage, structural details, interactive set dressing.
- **Background layer**: thematic scenery, silhouette architecture, skyline, weather, or atmospheric effects.

### 6.4 Environmental Readability Rules
- Avoid background clutter behind combat lanes.
- Keep destructible objects visually distinct from indestructible ones.
- Reserve bright highlights for interactive or dangerous objects.
- Use consistent floor contrast so players can read movement paths.

---

## 7. Tiles
### 7.1 Tile Philosophy
Tiles are the foundation of map readability. They must be modular, attractive, and easy to construct into clearly legible arenas.

### 7.2 Tile Types
Recommended tile categories include:
- Floor tiles.
- Edge or boundary tiles.
- Solid wall tiles.
- Breakable block tiles.
- Hazard tiles.
- Ice tiles.
- Decorative transition tiles.
- Interactive tiles such as switches, pads, or pressure plates.

### 7.3 Tile Style Rules
- Every tile set should have a clear base floor, accent layer, and boundary language.
- Tile outlines should be consistent and not overly thick.
- Breakable tiles should communicate fragility through color, cracks, or visual material difference.
- Hazard tiles should never resemble safe floor tiles.

### 7.4 Tile Modularity
Tiles should snap cleanly in grid-based construction. Corners, edges, and transitions must be authored so maps do not feel visually broken or repetitive.

### 7.5 Tile Variety
To keep maps fresh, each theme should include:
- Base tiles.
- Wall blocks.
- Breakables.
- Decorative trim.
- Hazard variants.
- Special icon or motif tiles tied to the map theme.

---

## 8. Lighting
### 8.1 Lighting Philosophy
Lighting should create mood and separation without destroying pixel clarity. It should feel modern but still respect the readability needs of a multiplayer arena game.

### 8.2 Lighting Style
- Use soft ambient gradients in the environment background.
- Add localized glow to pickups, hazards, explosions, and interactive elements.
- Use rim lighting or outline glow sparingly for hero characters or special states.
- Reserve strong bloom-like treatment for special moments only.

### 8.3 Lighting Rules
- The playfield must remain readable even when effects are active.
- Lighting should help separate foreground from background.
- Danger zones should feel brighter or hotter than safe zones where appropriate.
- Cold maps should use low-saturation blues and pale light sources.

### 8.4 Day / Mood Control
Lighting should support theme mood rather than realistic sun positioning. A map can be bright, dim, neon, eerie, or warm depending on its identity.

---

## 9. Particles
### 9.1 Particle Philosophy
Particles should support impact, motion, and atmosphere. They must never overwhelm the play area.

### 9.2 Particle Types
Recommended particles include:
- Pickup sparkles.
- Movement dust or streaks.
- Bomb fuse embers.
- Explosion fragments.
- Ice shards.
- Coin glints.
- Healing or shield motes.
- Critical hit or elimination bursts.
- Ambient thematic particles such as snow, ash, pollen, or neon motes.

### 9.3 Particle Rules
- Use few particles for repeated actions, more particles for rare or high-value events.
- Particles should support shape and color identity.
- Avoid tiny noisy particles that create visual static in the center of the screen.
- Each map can have a light ambient particle layer, but it must remain subtle.

### 9.4 Particle Shape Language
- Sparks: sharp, angular, energetic.
- Ice: crystalline, angular, pale.
- Coins: bright, small, clean flashes.
- Magic or ancient themes: soft motes and glyph-like fragments.
- Tech themes: pixel shards, data streaks, or electric dots.

---

## 10. Explosion Effects
### 10.1 Explosion Philosophy
Explosion effects are one of the most important moments in the game. They must be satisfying, instantly legible, and visually distinct from other hazards.

### 10.2 Explosion Design
An explosion should usually have:
- A bright core flash.
- A clear outward shape.
- A lingering hazard frame if applicable.
- Secondary particles or debris.
- Distinct animation timing for readability.

### 10.3 Explosion Readability Rules
- The blast boundary must be obvious.
- Players must be able to tell where the danger begins and ends.
- Explosion timing should be telegraphed through fuse, glow, or pulse cues.
- The effect should feel powerful without obscuring the battlefield for too long.

### 10.4 Explosion Variants
Different systems may use different explosion variants:
- Bomb detonations.
- Ice shatter bursts.
- Hazard eruptions.
- Chain reaction flashes.
- Objective completion pulses.

### 10.5 Explosion Palette
Explosions should usually lean toward:
- Orange and yellow for heat.
- Cyan and white for energy or freeze shatter.
- Coral or red for danger or combat intensity.
- Gold for reward or victory events.

---

## 11. UI Style
### 11.1 UI Art Direction
The UI should follow the same retro-modern language as the game: crisp panels, dark backgrounds, neon accents, and highly legible type and icons.

### 11.2 UI Surface Style
- Dark slate panels with clear borders.
- Slightly rounded square corners.
- Bright highlight edges for focus states.
- Pixel-styled embellishments for headers and badges.

### 11.3 UI Mood
- Arcade cabinet inspired.
- Clean and confident.
- Friendly to new players.
- Sharp enough for competitive users.

### 11.4 UI Readability Rules
- Primary actions must dominate visually.
- Secondary actions should be quieter and less saturated.
- Reward cards should feel celebratory but not cluttered.
- On small screens, UI must remain airy enough to avoid fatigue.

---

## 12. Icons
### 12.1 Icon Philosophy
Icons should be minimal, bold, and easy to decode in a fraction of a second.

### 12.2 Icon Style
- Geometric shapes with strong silhouette.
- Limited fine detail.
- Consistent stroke or edge treatment.
- Pixel-friendly construction.
- Accent color to indicate category or state.

### 12.3 Icon Categories
- Currency.
- Powerups.
- Rank symbols.
- Social actions.
- Settings.
- Combat status.
- Mission and reward badges.
- Map and mode icons.

### 12.4 Icon Rules
- Every icon should work at small size.
- Avoid decorative clutter inside the icon silhouette.
- Use shape first, color second.
- Motion can be used sparingly for hover or acquisition states.

---

## 13. Backgrounds
### 13.1 Background Philosophy
Backgrounds should establish world flavor without competing with gameplay. They should feel rich in theme, but restrained in the play area.

### 13.2 Background Layers
- Skybox or distant silhouette layer.
- Mid-distance scenic layer.
- Ground or support architecture.
- Atmospheric effects such as haze, particles, or moving light.

### 13.3 Background Rules
- High contrast should occur in the gameplay zone, not the far background.
- Busy background textures should be softened or darkened behind active play.
- Background motion should be slow enough not to distract.

### 13.4 Background Theming
Each map theme should have a background that reinforces narrative identity, such as city neon, forest canopy, volcanic smoke, or space silhouette.

---

## 14. Color Palette
### 14.1 Master Palette Direction
The overall game should use a dark neutral base with vivid theme accents and carefully controlled highlights.

### 14.2 Core Global Palette
- **Deep Navy**: `#0C1020`
- **Panel Slate**: `#151C33`
- **Highlight Slate**: `#223056`
- **Text Primary**: `#F4F7FF`
- **Text Secondary**: `#AAB3D6`
- **Muted Text**: `#6F789A`
- **Neon Cyan**: `#42E8F5`
- **Neon Mint**: `#57F287`
- **Arcade Yellow**: `#FFD84D`
- **Hot Coral**: `#FF5D7A`
- **Victory Gold**: `#F6C85F`
- **Defeat Blue-Violet**: `#6B7CFF`

### 14.3 Palette Strategy
- Use the global palette for UI and universal gameplay objects.
- Each theme should add its own accent palette.
- Avoid using too many saturated colors in the same field of view.
- Reserve the brightest values for rewards, hazards, and major gameplay cues.

### 14.4 Color Safety
- Use colorblind-safe contrasts for gameplay-critical states.
- Never rely on color alone for hazards, pickups, or status.
- Use shape, animation, and iconography alongside color.

## 15. Map Themes
Each map theme should feel like a complete art set, not a palette swap. The environment language, tile forms, props, lighting, and background silhouettes should all reinforce the theme.

---

## 15.1 Winter
### Theme Goal
Winter maps should feel cold, crisp, and strategically slippery. They should suggest frozen danger without becoming visually washed out.

### Visual Direction
- Pale blues, frosted whites, soft violets, and icy silver.
- Snow dust, frosty rim light, and soft drifting flakes.
- Crystalline surfaces and subtle reflective sheen.

### Tile Language
- Packed snow floor.
- Frosted stone edges.
- Ice block obstacles.
- Snow-covered walls and rooflines.

### Props and Backgrounds
- Icicles.
- Frozen lanterns.
- Distant blizzards or snowy treelines.
- Frozen bridges, towers, or harbor structures.

### Special Effects
- Breath-like mist in cold zones.
- Ice shatter sparks.
- Snow puffs from movement.

---

## 15.2 Forest
### Theme Goal
Forest maps should feel alive, layered, and slightly magical while still keeping the playfield clean.

### Visual Direction
- Deep greens, moss, bark brown, leaf gold, and soft cyan glow accents.
- Organic shapes with enough geometry to remain grid-friendly.
- Dappled light through canopy layers.

### Tile Language
- Mossy ground.
- Rooted path edges.
- Stone totems.
- Wooden barricades and dense shrubs.

### Props and Backgrounds
- Tree trunks.
- Canopy silhouettes.
- Fireflies or pollen motes.
- Ancient ruins embedded in the forest.

### Special Effects
- Leaf bursts from movement or destruction.
- Sap or pollen glints for pickups.
- Subtle wind-driven foliage motion.

---

- Conveyor strip tiles.
- Reinforced plates.

### Props and Backgrounds
- Pistons.
- Gears.
- Overhead cranes.

- Control panels and glowing indicators.

### Special Effects
- Steam puffs.
- Sparks from machinery.
- Hydraulic impact flashes.
- Small electrical arcs for hazard zones.

## 17. VFX Integration with Art
VFX must always respect the underlying pixel style.

### VFX Rules
- Effects should use pixel-consistent shapes.
- Big effects may use layered pixel shapes and simple glow passes.
- Avoid realistic smoke or modern painterly effects unless stylized to match the game.
- Special effects should enhance action readability and never hide player intent.

### VFX Hierarchy
- Small persistent effects: dust, motes, ambient particles.
- Medium effects: pickups, status effects, small explosions.
- Large effects: bomb detonations, map hazards, objective events.

## 18. Production Notes for Pixel Artists
### 18.1 Asset Consistency
All artists should follow the same pixel grid, outline logic, and contrast discipline.

### 18.2 Sprite Read Tests
Every new sprite should be checked for:
- Recognition at gameplay zoom.
- Motion clarity.
- Color separation from the floor.
- Visibility during combat effects.

### 18.3 Map Read Tests
Every environment set should be checked for:
- Safe tile readability.
- Hazard readability.
- Pickup visibility.
- Bomb and explosion contrast.
- Mobile screen clarity.

### 18.4 Asset Priorities
The most important assets are:
- Player sprites.
- Bombs and hazards.
- Pickups.
- Key environmental tiles.
- UI icons.
- Map-specific landmarks.

---

## 19. Final Art Direction Statement
Pixel Panic should feel like a high-quality arcade battleground rendered in modern pixel art: colorful, readable, energetic, and strongly themed. Characters should be expressive but compact. Environments should be memorable but never noisy. Lighting and effects should enhance tension and emotion without harming clarity.

If a visual choice improves atmosphere but harms competitive readability, it should be rejected. If a visual choice supports instant recognition, strong theme identity, and satisfying combat feedback, it should be prioritized.
